FIGURE 1
================================================================================

LEGEND:
Fig. 1 Study landscape for Acropora palmata demographic data across the
Caribbean. (a) Geographic distribution of observations. Bubble size is
proportional to the number of observations at each site. Study locations were
geocoded to region centroids. (b) Data availability matrix showing sample
sizes across 5 size classes (SC1-SC5) and Caribbean regions. Cell fill
indicates sample size (n); grey cells indicate no data. Regions are ordered by
total sample size.

METHODS:
We compiled individual-level survival and growth records for A. palmata from
published studies and monitoring programs across the Caribbean. Colony size was
standardized to two-dimensional live tissue area (cm2), following the planar
live-area convention used elsewhere in the project. Data availability was
cross-tabulated across the five canonical size classes (SC1: 0-10 cm2;
SC2: 10-100 cm2; SC3: 100-900 cm2; SC4: 900-4,000 cm2; SC5: >4,000 cm2) and
the sampled Caribbean regions.

RESULTS:
The synthesis currently spans 14,160 observations from 7 individual-level
studies across 13 Caribbean regions. Coverage is geographically uneven:
Florida provides the largest share of observations, followed by Curacao, with
substantially smaller contributions from Navassa, Mexico, the US Virgin
Islands, and the Dominican Republic. Florida has the most complete size-class
coverage, whereas several non-Florida regions have sparse representation in
only two or three size classes. Large adults (SC5) remain poorly represented
outside Florida, Curacao, and Navassa even though they exert the greatest
population-viability leverage.

FIGURE 2
================================================================================

LEGEND:
Fig. 2 Size-dependent vital rates for Acropora palmata. (a) Annual survival
probability as a function of colony live tissue area (cm2, log10 scale). Solid
blue line shows the fitted generalized additive model (GAM; binomial family,
REML smoothness selection). Shaded ribbon indicates the 95% confidence
interval. Points show binned survival proportions. (b) Relative growth rate
(RGR) as a function of colony live tissue area. Solid teal line shows the
fitted GAM (Gaussian family). Orange points show binned median RGR values and
grey points show individual observations. The vertical dashed orange line marks
the detected RGR threshold at 36.9 cm2, and the shaded orange band shows the
cluster-bootstrap interval (36.9-38.9 cm2). Dashed horizontal line indicates
zero growth. (c) Annual survival by size class across all available studies.
Points show study-level survival estimates by size class, colored by population
type and shaped by data tier. Point size is proportional to sample size. Bold
black diamonds show pooled survival by size class.

METHODS:
We modeled the relationship between colony size and annual survival using a GAM
with a binomial error distribution and a smooth term for log-transformed colony
size. The growth panel used a Gaussian GAM fit to relative growth rate, with
impossible growth values removed and extreme RGR outliers trimmed before model
fitting. For both responses, we used the Detmer et al. (2025) nonlinearity
gate and threshold framework to classify functional form and identify supported
thresholds. Panel c summarizes annualized survival by the five canonical size
classes using the integrated individual-level and summary-level data products.

RESULTS:
Both survival and growth are size dependent, but they differ in form and
strength. Survival shows a supported sigmoidal relationship with colony size,
with a recommended threshold near 7,498 cm2 and broad leave-one-study-out
uncertainty (2,136-8,748 cm2), indicating that the survival inflection is real
but weakly stable across the available study mix. Relative growth rate shows a
much sharper nonlinear transition: the supported threshold is 36.9 cm2 and the
bootstrap interval is tight (36.9-38.9 cm2), indicating that the steepest
decline in proportional growth occurs early in ontogeny. Across the size-class
synthesis, pooled annual survival is lowest and most variable in the smaller
and mid-sized classes, and highest in large adults, but variability in SC2-SC4
reflects mixed disturbance history and population composition rather than size
alone.

FIGURE 3
================================================================================

LEGEND:
Fig. 3 Caribbean-wide survival synthesis for Acropora palmata. (a) Forest plot
of annual survival probability from the expanded meta-analysis (k = 17 studies,
22 effects, N = 8,805). Studies are grouped by population type. Point size is
proportional to random-effects weight. Diamonds show subgroup and overall
pooled estimates. The dashed vertical line marks the overall pooled survival.
The shaded band indicates the 95% prediction interval. (b) Geographic
variation in annual survival across Caribbean regions. Points show individual
study estimates and diamonds with confidence intervals show pooled regional
estimates for regions with multiple contributing studies.

METHODS:
We conducted a two-tier random-effects meta-analysis combining individual-level
and summary-level survival effects. Effect sizes were computed as proportional
log-odds and fit with REML estimation and the Knapp-Hartung adjustment.
Non-annual survival rates were annualized assuming constant hazard. Panel b
uses the same meta-analytic framework to summarize regions with multiple
contributing effects.

RESULTS:
The expanded synthesis yields a pooled annual survival of 78.0% (95% CI:
70.1-84.3%), but the 95% prediction interval is wide (39.5-95.1%), consistent
with extreme heterogeneity across studies and regions (I2 = 97.2%). Natural
colony effects show higher pooled survival than restoration-fragment effects
(84.1% vs 74.5%), but the difference is not statistically significant and
should be interpreted as heterogeneous rather than universal. Geographic
variation remains substantial even within population type, reinforcing that the
pooled value is a conditional Caribbean summary, not a region-independent
constant.

FIGURE 4
================================================================================

LEGEND:
Fig. 4 Population viability assessment for Acropora palmata. (a) Estimated 5x5
Lefkovitch transition matrix. Cell values show annual transition probabilities
between size classes. Fill color indicates probability magnitude. Diagonal
elements represent stasis, below-diagonal transitions represent growth, and
above-diagonal transitions represent retrogression. (b) Elasticity
decomposition by size class and vital rate type. Stacked bars show the
contribution of stasis, growth, retrogression, and fragmentation to total
elasticity for each size class. (c) Bootstrap distribution of population growth
rate (`lambda`); bars are colored by whether each replicate indicates decline
(`lambda < 1`) or growth (`lambda >= 1`). (d) Leave-one-study-out sensitivity
analysis for `lambda`.

METHODS:
We constructed a five-class Lefkovitch transition matrix using the prepared
natural-colony transition data and the size classes adopted throughout the
project. The dominant eigenvalue was used as the deterministic population
growth rate (`lambda`). Elasticity analysis decomposed the sensitivity of
`lambda` into stasis, growth, retrogression, and fragmentation components.
Bootstrap uncertainty was estimated via hierarchical resampling with 2,000
valid replicates, and leave-one-study-out sensitivity analysis was used to
assess dependence on individual study contributions.

RESULTS:
The transition matrix shows that stasis dominates all size classes, but
retrogression is not negligible. A dedicated shrinkage synthesis indicates that
39.4% of matrix-compatible growth records involve tissue loss, and observed
retrogression probabilities are 15.2% in SC2, 16.8% in SC3, 17.2% in SC4, and
9.1% in SC5. Large-adult persistence remains the dominant viability lever:
SC5-to-SC5 stasis accounts for 57.4% of matrix-cell elasticity, and aggregated
SC5 survival elasticity is 58.8%. The current deterministic `lambda` is 0.967,
with a bootstrap 95% interval of 0.828-1.059 and an 86.0% bootstrap
probability of decline. Leave-one-study-out sensitivity spans `lambda` =
0.882-0.993, showing that the viability estimate remains conditional on a small
set of natural-colony transition datasets.

SUPPLEMENTARY FIGURE S16
================================================================================

LEGEND:
Fig. S16 Shrinkage and retrogression in Acropora palmata. Panels summarize
shrinkage frequency, tissue loss magnitude, and observed retrogression
probabilities by size class, using the matrix-compatible subset for shrinkage
metrics and observed transitions for retrogression estimates.

METHODS:
Shrinkage was defined as negative annual change in live tissue area among
surviving colonies. The main summary uses the matrix-compatible subset of the
cleaned growth data; an all-clean sensitivity set is also retained in the
output files. Retrogression was quantified directly from observed size-class
transitions in the matrix-compatible data.

RESULTS:
Shrinkage is common rather than exceptional. In the matrix-compatible subset,
39.4% of growth records involve tissue loss. Shrinkage frequency rises from
18.2% in SC1 to 47.5% in SC5. Retrogression probabilities are 15.2% in SC2,
16.8% in SC3, 17.2% in SC4, and 9.1% in SC5, showing that many colonies
survive while moving backward demographically after partial mortality.

SUPPLEMENTARY FIGURE S17
================================================================================

LEGEND:
Fig. S17 Disturbance-by-size interaction for Acropora palmata demographic
performance. Panels compare size-dependent survival and positive-growth
probability across three disturbance states: no curated disturbance,
context-only pressure, and acute baseline-exclusion exposure. The figure is
restricted to natural colonies to avoid confounding with restoration subtype.

METHODS:
Prepared interval-level disturbance assignments were grouped into three states:
none, context-only pressure, and acute baseline-exclusion events. Interaction
models tested whether the relationship between log colony size and demographic
performance differed across those disturbance states.

RESULTS:
The survival interaction is strong and manuscript-relevant: the overall
size-by-disturbance comparison is significant (`LRT p = 6.93e-4`), and the
size effect becomes steeper under both context-only pressure and acute
baseline-exclusion exposure. The growth-side interaction is weaker and should
be interpreted more cautiously: the model-comparison test is supportive
(`LRT p = 0.010`), but the individual interaction coefficients are not
consistently strong.

SUPPLEMENTARY FIGURE S19
================================================================================

LEGEND:
Fig. S19 Restoration subtype sensitivity for Acropora palmata. Survival and
growth summaries are shown for the defensible restoration subtypes identified
from study metadata: natural fragments, nursery outplants, nursery outplants
re-cemented after fragmentation, and outplanted colonies.

METHODS:
Broad restoration-fragment labels were decomposed into biologically distinct
subtypes using project metadata and source-study descriptions. Subtype-specific
survival and growth summaries were then computed across the subtype-coded
subset.

RESULTS:
The subtype-coded dataset contains 5,079 records across 5 studies. Natural
fragments have weighted survival of 0.871 (n = 3,968), nursery outplants 0.587
(n = 1,012), nursery outplants re-cemented after fragmentation 0.811 (n = 53),
and outplanted colonies 0.652 (n = 46). Excluding natural fragments reduces
mean survival across subtype-coded records from 0.812 to 0.600, showing that
the generic label “restoration fragment” obscures biologically important
differences among intervention types.


================================================================================
METHODOLOGICAL LIMITATIONS
================================================================================

Several methodological heterogeneities should be kept explicit in the paper.

SIZE MEASUREMENT:
Studies used different approaches to measure colony size, including live
planar area, photo-estimated area, diameter-based area estimates, and
length-width products without explicit live-tissue adjustment. The analysis
prioritized live tissue area when available and used study-level harmonization
rules elsewhere. Mixed models and study-level summaries absorb some of this
measurement heterogeneity, but not all of it.

MORTALITY DEFINITIONS:
Whole-colony mortality criteria differed across studies. Some programs treated
disappearance as mortality, some required complete tissue loss, and some did
not report explicit thresholds. These differences are one reason pooled
survival should be interpreted as heterogeneous and conditional rather than as
a single precise demographic constant.

MONITORING INTERVALS:
Monitoring intervals are approximately annual in most datasets, but some
intervals are shorter or longer. Survival was annualized assuming constant
hazard and multi-year growth intervals were restricted in matrix-model
construction to reduce bias from linear-growth assumptions over long gaps.

POPULATION MATRIX COMPOSITION:
The transition matrix is built from the natural-colony subset and remains
Florida heavy, especially for large adults. SC5 stasis is still the dominant
elasticity component (57.4% of matrix-cell elasticity), but the resulting
`lambda` estimate is conditional on the available study mix and not a
Caribbean-wide universal growth rate.

NATURAL VS. RESTORATION CONFOUNDING:
Population type remains strongly entangled with study identity, region,
handling history, and available size range. The expanded meta-analysis includes
10 natural and 12 restoration effects, but broad restoration contrasts should
not be interpreted as a single causal effect. The restoration subtype analysis
shows why: nursery outplants, re-cemented outplants, outplanted colonies, and
natural fragments do not behave as one biological category.

KNOWN UNKNOWNS:
Recruitment and fecundity are not estimated with the same strength as survival
and growth. The current literature supports maturity thresholds, early-life
bottlenecks, and reproductive context, but not a fully transferable Caribbean
fecundity schedule or a disturbance-adjusted recruitment module.
