LEGEND:
Fig. 1 Study landscape for Acropora palmata demographic data across the
Caribbean. (a) Geographic distribution of observations. Bubble size is
proportional to the number of observations at each site (6 studies, 6 regions,
N = 9,456 total observations). Study locations were geocoded to region
centroids. (b) Data availability matrix showing sample sizes across 5 size

grey cells indicate no data. Regions ordered by total sample size.

METHODS:
We compiled individual-level survival and growth records for A. palmata from
published studies and monitoring programs across the Caribbean. Colony size was
measured as two-dimensional planar live tissue area (cm2), calculated as
length x width x proportion live tissue following Vardi et al. (2012). Study
locations were geocoded to region centroids. Data availability was assessed by
cross-tabulating survival records across the 5 canonical size classes
(SC1: 0-10 cm2; SC2: 10-100 cm2; SC3: 100-900 cm2; SC4: 900-4,000 cm2;
SC5: >4,000 cm2) and Caribbean regions.

RESULTS:
The synthesis encompasses 9,456 observations from 6 studies conducted across 6
Caribbean regions between 2004 and 2024. The data are geographically uneven:
Florida contributed the largest share of observations (n = 4,031; 78% from the
NOAA National Coral Reef Monitoring Program), followed by Curacao (n = 856),
Navassa (n = 102), Mexico (n = 52), USVI (n = 46), and the Dominican Republic
(n = 44). Florida had the most complete size-class coverage, with observations
in all five classes. Several regions (Mexico, USVI, Dominican Republic) had
data in only 2-3 size classes, and SC5 (>4,000 cm2) — the size class with the
highest elasticity (54.8%) — had no data outside Florida, Curacao, and Navassa.

FIGURE 2
================================================================================

LEGEND:
Fig. 2 Size-dependent vital rates for Acropora palmata. (a) Annual survival
probability as a function of colony live tissue area (cm2, log10 scale). Solid
blue line shows the fitted generalized additive model (GAM; binomial family,
k = 4 basis dimensions, REML smoothness selection). Shaded ribbon indicates the
95% confidence interval. Points show binned survival proportions (25 equal-width
bins on the log scale, minimum n = 5 per bin). Rug marks along the top axis
indicate colonies that survived; rug marks along the bottom axis (orange)
indicate colonies that died (n = 3,926 natural colonies from 2 studies; of
these, 99% are from NOAA NCRMP). (b) Relative growth rate (RGR) as a function
of colony live tissue area. Solid teal line shows the fitted GAM (Gaussian
family, k = 4). Orange points show binned median RGR values (25 bins). Grey
points show individual observations (n = 3,034). Dashed horizontal line
indicates zero growth. The vertical dashed orange line marks the detected RGR
threshold at 36 cm2 (T2: most negative second derivative; Detmer et al. 2025),
and the shaded orange band shows the 95% leave-one-study-out bootstrap
confidence interval (33-396 cm2). (c) Annual survival by size class across all
available studies. Points show study-level survival estimates (SC1-SC5), colored
by population type (blue = natural, orange = restoration) and shaped by data
tier (circles = individual, triangles = summary). Point size proportional to
sample size. Bold black line with diamonds shows pooled survival. k annotations
show contributing studies per class. Data from 15 studies across 10 Caribbean
regions (N = 9,260).

METHODS:
We modeled the relationship between colony size and annual survival probability
using a generalized additive model (GAM) with a binomial error distribution and
logit link function, fitted via restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The smooth
term for log-transformed colony size used a thin-plate regression spline with
k = 4 basis dimensions. Analysis was restricted to natural (non-fragment)
colonies. Binned survival proportions were calculated by dividing the log-size
range into 25 equal-width bins (bins with <5 observations excluded). Predictions
and 95% confidence intervals were generated on the link (logit) scale and
back-transformed to the probability scale. We modeled the size-RGR relationship
with a GAM using a Gaussian error distribution and the same smooth structure.
Relative growth rate was calculated as annual absolute growth (cm2 yr-1) divided
by initial colony size (cm2). Records with impossible growth (tissue loss
exceeding 110% of initial size) were excluded, and the top and bottom 0.5% of
RGR values were trimmed. Binned medians were calculated from 25 equal-width bins
on the log scale. We applied the nonlinearity gate and threshold detection
framework of Detmer et al. (2025) to the size-RGR relationship. The gate test
confirmed significant nonlinearity (delta AICc = 58.4, threshold = 2). The
functional form was classified as sigmoidal, and the recommended threshold
definition (T2: most negative second derivative) was used. Threshold uncertainty
was estimated via leave-one-study-out cross-validation across 6 study folds.
For the size-class synthesis (panel c), individual-level survival data from 6
studies were binned into 5 size classes following Vardi et al. (2012).
Summary-level data from 9 additional studies were assigned to size classes based
on reported mean colony sizes or midpoints of reported size ranges. Non-annual
survival rates were annualized assuming constant hazard (S_annual = S_raw^(1/t)).
Studies representing post-settlement recruits (<1 cm2) or laboratory
micro-fragments were excluded. Pooled estimates were computed using
sqrt(n)-weighted means on the logit scale with back-transformation to the
probability scale.

RESULTS:
Annual survival probability increased with colony size from approximately 54%
for the smallest colonies (~1 cm2) to 96% for the largest (>10,000 cm2). The
survival GAM explained 7.3% of the deviance (R^2 = 5.8%), indicating that while
the size-survival relationship is statistically significant, colony size alone
is a weak predictor of individual survival. The relationship was monotonically
increasing with no evidence of a nonlinear threshold (Detmer et al. 2025 gate
test: delta AICc = 0.83, below the threshold of 2). Relative growth rate
declined monotonically with colony size. Small colonies (~5 cm2) had a predicted
RGR of 0.66 yr-1, declining to 0.06 yr-1 for the largest colonies (~10,000
cm2). The RGR GAM explained 9.2% of the variance (R^2 = 9.2%). The Detmer et
al. (2025) nonlinearity gate confirmed a significant departure from linearity
(delta AICc = 58.4). The detected RGR threshold (T2) was 36 cm2 (95% LOSO CI:
33-396 cm2), indicating that the steepest decline in proportional growth occurs
around this colony size. Pooled annual survival increases with size class:
SC1 = 89.4%, SC2 = 78.0%, SC3 = 76.3%, SC4 = 90.2%, SC5 = 97.1% (k = 4-10
studies per class). The SC2-SC3 dip reflects higher representation of
restoration fragment studies in these size classes, which show lower survival.
SC5 (>4,000 cm2) survival is consistently high across all 4 contributing
studies, confirming that large adult persistence is the dominant demographic
process.

FIGURE 3
================================================================================

LEGEND:
Fig. 3 Caribbean-wide survival synthesis for Acropora palmata. (a) Forest plot
of annual survival probability from the expanded meta-analysis (k = 16 studies,
20 effects, N = 6,560). Studies are grouped by population type: natural colonies
(k = 9, blue) and restoration fragments (k = 11, orange). Within each group, studies are
ordered by survival rate. Point estimates with 95% confidence intervals are
shown for each study. Point size is proportional to random-effects weight. Shape
indicates data tier: circles for individual-level data, triangles for
summary-level data. Diamonds show subgroup pooled estimates and the overall
random-effects pooled estimate (bottom). The dashed vertical line marks the
overall pooled survival (78.4%). The light blue shaded band indicates the 95%
prediction interval (39.0-95.4%). Heterogeneity: I^2 = 96.4% [93.3%, 98.6%],
tau^2 = 0.646. (b) Geographic variation in annual survival across 9 Caribbean
regions. Points show individual study estimates colored by population type.
Diamonds with CIs show pooled regional estimates (k >= 2). Dashed vertical line
at overall pooled (78.4%).

METHODS:
We conducted a two-tier random-effects meta-analysis combining individual-level
data from 6 studies (Tier 1, contributing 7 effects after splitting NOAA by
region) with study-level survival estimates from 10 additional published studies
(Tier 2, contributing 13 effects after splitting Vardi 2011 by region). Effect sizes were computed as
proportional log-odds (PLO) using the escalc function in metafor (R). The
random-effects model used restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation with
the Knapp-Hartung small-sample adjustment. Non-annual survival rates were
annualized assuming constant hazard. Studies representing post-settlement
recruits or laboratory micro-fragments were excluded. Vardi (2011) was split
into 3 regional effects (Jamaica, Puerto Rico, Virgin Gorda) as geographically
independent populations. Regional pooled estimates (panel b) were computed for
regions with k >= 2 contributing studies using the same random-effects
framework.

RESULTS:
The expanded meta-analysis (k = 16 studies, 20 effects) yielded a pooled annual
survival of 78.4%, but the 95% prediction interval (39.0-95.4%) indicates that
a new study could plausibly observe survival anywhere in this range — reflecting
extreme heterogeneity (I^2 = 96.4%). The 95% confidence interval for the pooled
estimate was 69.9-85.0%. Natural colony effects (k = 9) showed higher pooled
survival (84.6%, CI: 71.4-92.3%) than restoration fragment effects (k = 11;
76.0%, CI: 65.4-84.2%), but the 8.5 percentage point difference was not
statistically significant (Q-moderator p = 0.236). Splitting NOAA by region
(FL Keys, Curacao, Navassa) and Vardi 2011 by region (Jamaica, PR, Virgin
Gorda) increased natural colony representation from k = 1 (NOAA only) to
k = 9 effects across multiple regions, modeled with a three-level
random-effects structure to account for within-study correlation.
Regionally, survival estimates ranged widely across the 9 Caribbean regions,
with pooled estimates available for regions with multiple contributing studies.
The geographic pattern shows substantial variation that is not fully explained
by population type, reinforcing the extreme heterogeneity detected in the
overall meta-analysis.

LEGEND:
Fig. 4 Population viability assessment for Acropora palmata. (a) Estimated 5x5
Lefkovitch transition matrix. Cell values show annual transition probabilities
between size classes. Fill color indicates probability magnitude (viridis
scale). Diagonal elements represent stasis (persistence within the same size
class). Below-diagonal transitions represent growth; above-diagonal represent
retrogression. (b) Elasticity decomposition by size class and vital rate type.
Stacked bars show the proportional contribution of stasis (staying in same size
class), growth (transition to larger class), retrogression (shrinkage to smaller
class), and fragmentation to total elasticity for each size class (SC1-SC5).
SC5 stasis (large adult persistence) accounts for 54.8% of total elasticity.
(c) Bootstrap distribution of the population growth rate (lambda). Bars are
colored by whether each bootstrap replicate indicates population decline
(orange, lambda < 1) or growth (blue, lambda >= 1). Solid grey vertical line
marks replacement (lambda = 1); dashed dark blue line marks the deterministic
lambda estimate. (d) Leave-one-study-out (LOSO) sensitivity analysis. Each
point shows the estimated lambda when one study is excluded from the transition
matrix. Dashed vertical line marks the full-model lambda (1.001); dotted line
marks replacement (lambda = 1). Only two studies (NOAA and Mendoza-Quiroz)
contribute to the natural-colony transition matrix; LOSO range is
0.991-1.011.

METHODS:
We constructed a 5 x 5 Lefkovitch (size-structured) transition matrix following
Vardi et al. (2012), with size classes SC1-SC5 (SC1: 0-10 cm2; SC2: 10-100 cm2;
SC3: 100-900 cm2; SC4: 900-4,000 cm2; SC5: >4,000 cm2). Transition
probabilities were estimated as the observed frequency of transitions between
size classes across annual monitoring intervals, restricted to natural colonies.
The complete transition matrix is displayed as a heatmap (panel a) to visualize
the relative magnitude of all inter-class transitions, stasis probabilities,
and retrogression rates simultaneously. The dominant eigenvalue of the
transition matrix (lambda) was computed as the deterministic population growth
rate. Elasticity analysis decomposed the proportional sensitivity of lambda into
contributions from stasis, growth, retrogression, and fragmentation transitions
for each size class. Fragmentation rates were derived from Vardi (2011), with
the SC1_V class split proportionally into SC1 (25%) and SC2 (75%) by range
width. Bootstrap uncertainty was estimated using hierarchical resampling
(study-level, then observation-level within study) with 2,000 replicates,
computing lambda for each replicate matrix. The probability of decline was
calculated as the proportion of bootstrap replicates with lambda < 1.
Leave-one-study-out (LOSO) sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially
excluding each study and recomputing the transition matrix and lambda.

RESULTS:
The transition matrix (panel a) reveals that stasis dominates all size classes,
with diagonal probabilities ranging from 0.55 (SC2) to 0.89 (SC5). Growth
transitions are most frequent from adjacent size classes, and retrogression
(shrinkage) is rare except for SC3-to-SC2 and SC4-to-SC3 transitions.
Elasticity analysis (panel b) confirmed that SC5 stasis (large adult
persistence) accounts for 54.8% of total elasticity, with SC4 stasis
contributing an additional 11.4%. Growth transitions collectively account for
14.5% and fragmentation for 7.8%. This identifies protection of large colonies
as the most effective conservation target: a 1% improvement in SC5 survival has
approximately 4x more impact on population growth than equivalent improvements
to other vital rates.

The deterministic population growth rate was lambda = 1.001, indicating a
near-stable population. The bootstrap 95% confidence interval was wide
[0.863, 1.035], spanning both decline and growth scenarios. The probability of
population decline (P(lambda < 1)) was 65.1% (1519 valid bootstrap replicates
of 2000 attempted). LOSO sensitivity analysis showed that lambda was relatively
stable: excluding NOAA gave lambda = 1.011, and excluding Mendoza-Quiroz gave
lambda = 0.991 (LOSO range = 0.020). Only two studies (NOAA and Mendoza-Quiroz)
contribute to the natural-colony transition matrix, limiting LOSO to these two
studies.

Fig. S2 Data certainty matrix for Acropora palmata survival records across size
classes and Caribbean regions. Cell values indicate sample sizes. Fill color

METHODS:
We assessed data certainty by cross-tabulating survival records across size
class and region combinations, classifying each cell by sample size thresholds.
Certainty levels were defined as: High (n >= 300), Moderate (n = 100-299),
Low (n = 30-99), Very low (n = 1-29), and No data (n = 0). These thresholds
reflect the minimum sample sizes needed for stable parameter estimates in
binomial models.

RESULTS:
Of the 30 size class x region cells in the data matrix, only 5 (17%) achieved
High certainty (all in Florida), 3 (10%) were Moderate, 3 (10%) were Low, 13
(43%) were Very low, and 6 (20%) had no data at all. Florida had the most
complete coverage, with High certainty across all five size classes except SC1
(Moderate). Curacao had Moderate-to-High certainty for SC3-SC5 but Very low
for SC1. All other regions (Navassa, Mexico, USVI, Dominican Republic) had
Very low certainty or no data across most size classes. Notably, SC5 (>4,000
cm2) — the size class with the highest elasticity contribution (54.8%) — had
no data outside Florida, Curacao, and Navassa, highlighting a critical
mismatch between conservation importance and data availability. SC4 and SC5
had no observations in Mexico, USVI, or the Dominican Republic.

restoration fragments have data. This range spans less than 2% of the full

METHODS:
We restricted the comparison to the shared size range (~11-202 cm2), defined
as the intersection of the 1st-99th percentile ranges of log-transformed colony
size for natural colonies and restoration fragments. This constraint ensures
that any comparison occurs only where both population types have observations.
Population type was classified based on study metadata: colonies from natural
reef surveys were classified as "Natural colony" and transplanted or nursery-
reared fragments as "Restoration fragment." For each study, we fitted a
logistic regression (GLM, binomial family, logit link) of annual survival on
log10-transformed colony size, and an ordinary least-squares regression of
relative growth rate on log10-transformed colony size. Each study is shown in
its own facet panel, with fitted lines and 95% confidence ribbons computed on
the link scale and back-transformed (survival) or displayed directly (RGR).
Individual survival observations are shown as jittered points at 0 (died) and
1 (survived). We excluded NOAA "fragment" records because cross-referencing
coral IDs revealed that the majority (59 of 73, 81%) also appeared as natural
colonies in the same dataset. Study identity is encoded as the primary visual
channel (distinct color per study, separate panels) because study identity is
nearly perfectly confounded with population type: natural colony data derive
almost exclusively from NOAA NCRMP (99% of natural colony records), while
restoration fragment data come from four separate studies, each with different
geographic locations, monitoring protocols, and fragment handling histories.

RESULTS:
Within the shared size range (11-202 cm2), NOAA NCRMP natural colonies showed
77.5% annual survival (n = 1,063), with logistic regression indicating
increasing survival from ~67% at 11 cm2 to ~83% at 202 cm2. Pausch et al.
restoration fragments showed 57.5% survival (n = 966), with survival declining
from ~68% to ~47% across the same range, yielding a ~20 percentage point gap
between these two largest studies. However, the four smaller studies exhibited
substantial heterogeneity: FUNDEMAR survival (88%; n = 43) exceeded the NOAA
estimate, while USGS USVI (65%; n = 46) fell between NOAA and Pausch. Kuffner
(81%; n = 52) also exceeded Pausch, and Mendoza-Quiroz natural colonies showed
100% survival (n = 35) — although this small sample with no mortality yields a
degenerate logistic fit. Within-restoration heterogeneity (31 percentage points
between Pausch 57.5% and FUNDEMAR 88.4%) exceeded the between-type difference,
confirming that population type cannot be disentangled from study-level
variation. For growth, NOAA colonies showed declining RGR from ~0.6 yr-1 at
11 cm2 to ~0.3 yr-1 at 202 cm2 (median = 0.37, n = 663). Pausch fragments
showed higher RGR across the range (median = 0.98, n = 525), declining from
~1.8 to ~0.4 yr-1. Restoration study medians ranged from 0.83 yr-1 (Kuffner)
to 1.57 yr-1 (FUNDEMAR), while Mendoza-Quiroz natural colonies showed a median
RGR of 1.05 yr-1 — within the restoration range — further illustrating the
confounding between study methodology and population type.

================================================================================
METHODOLOGICAL LIMITATIONS
================================================================================

Several methodological heterogeneities across studies should be considered when
interpreting these results.

SIZE MEASUREMENT:
Studies used different approaches to measure colony size. NOAA NCRMP and Pausch
et al. (2018) measured length x width x proportion live tissue. USGS USVI and
Kuffner et al. (2020) used photo-estimated planar area. FUNDEMAR measured
length x width without live tissue adjustment. Mendoza-Quiroz et al. (2023)
used diameter squared at some sites. The analysis prioritized live tissue area
when available and assumed 100% tissue cover otherwise. Study random effects in
mixed models absorb systematic differences across measurement methods.

MORTALITY DEFINITIONS:
Mortality was defined differently across studies. NOAA NCRMP classified
mortality as complete tissue loss or colony disappearance. Pausch et al. (2018)
treated exclusion from subsequent monitoring as mortality. USGS USVI required
no live tissue remaining. Kuffner et al. (2020) used a threshold of >=50%
tissue mortality, though photographic review confirmed all classified deaths
showed 100% mortality. Mendoza-Quiroz et al. (2023) and FUNDEMAR did not state
explicit mortality criteria. These differences could introduce systematic bias
in survival estimates, particularly the Pausch et al. approach of treating
loss-to-follow-up as mortality.

MONITORING INTERVALS:
Most monitoring intervals were approximately annual, but NOAA Navassa data
spanned 2.5-3 years and some NOAA Florida records exceeded 2 years. Survival
was annualized as S_annual = S_raw^(1/t) in the transition matrix. Growth
rates were pre-annualized in the standardized datasets. For the transition
matrix, growth data were restricted to 0.5-1.5 year intervals to avoid bias
from assuming linear growth over multi-year periods.

POPULATION MATRIX COMPOSITION:
The transition matrix was restricted to natural colonies, making it heavily
dependent on NOAA NCRMP data (which provides 99% of natural colony
observations). SC5 stasis — the parameter with the highest elasticity (54.8%)
— derives entirely from NOAA data across Florida, Curacao, and Navassa. No
non-NOAA study monitored colonies in the SC5 size range (>4,000 cm2). The
bootstrap confidence interval for lambda [0.863, 1.035] includes replacement
(lambda = 1), so population decline cannot be confirmed at the 95% level,
though 65.1% of replicates indicate decline. The bootstrap failure rate (481 of
2000 replicates invalid) reflects the small number of studies (k = 6) in
hierarchical resampling; see Fig. 4c for the specific counts of valid vs total
replicates.

NATURAL VS. RESTORATION CONFOUNDING:
Population type is nearly perfectly confounded with study identity in the
individual-level data: natural colony records derive almost exclusively from
NOAA NCRMP (99%), while restoration fragment data come from four separate
studies with different geographic locations, monitoring protocols, colony sizes,
and temporal coverage. The expanded meta-analysis (k = 16 studies, 20 effects) includes 9 natural
colony effects, yielding an 8.5 percentage-point difference (84.6% vs 76.0%,
p = 0.236) that is not statistically significant.
Within-restoration heterogeneity (31 percentage points between Pausch et al.
57.5% and FUNDEMAR 88.4%) exceeds the between-type difference, and
Mendoza-Quiroz natural colonies showed 100% survival (n = 35) within the
restoration study range. The apparent natural-vs-restoration difference should
not be interpreted as a causal effect of population type.
