# Methods: Literature Search, Data Compilation, and Meta-Analysis

This synthesis has a single parent goal: to estimate how *Acropora palmata* demographic performance varies with colony size across the Caribbean, and what that means for population viability under real disturbance regimes and for restoration decision-making. The analytical scope therefore extends beyond pooled survival estimation to include nonlinear size dependence, size-structured population modeling, disturbance overlays through time and space, natural-versus-restoration comparison, and explicit treatment of heterogeneity and uncertainty.

## 2.1 Literature search and study selection

We conducted a systematic compilation of all published demographic data (survival, growth, fragmentation) for *Acropora palmata* across the Caribbean, following PRISMA-EcoEvo guidelines for ecology and evolutionary biology (O'Dea et al. 2021). The search followed a two-phase protocol; full details are provided in the PRISMA-style protocol in the Supplementary Materials.

Search reporting follows the PRISMA-S extension for search strategies (Rethlefsen et al. 2021). Databases not searched included Scopus (substantial overlap with Web of Science for marine ecology journals), BIOSIS Previews, Zoological Record, and Aquatic Sciences and Fisheries Abstracts. The search strategy was not peer-reviewed by an information specialist; however, saturation analysis via Elicit (298 papers across Semantic Scholar's 125M+ corpus, recovering all 17 published included studies) provides evidence of search completeness.

Records identified across databases were manually deduplicated based on title, author, and year. No formal deduplication software was used.

The original screening was conducted by a single reviewer (RD). The expanded search served as a partial second-screener check.

**Original search.** The initial literature compilation was conducted between June and December 2025. Studies were identified through four channels: (1) data repository mining of NOAA NCEI, NOAA InPort, USGS CMGDS, and USGS ScienceBase; (2) bibliographic database searches using Google Scholar with five structured search strings combining the base query `("Acropora palmata" OR "elkhorn coral")` with terms targeting survival/mortality, growth, recruitment/fecundity, restoration demography, and long-term monitoring; (3) forward and backward citation chaining from key studies (Vardi 2011; Williams and Miller 2012; Lirman 2003); and (4) direct data sharing from restoration practitioners (FUNDEMAR, Dominican Republic). For each set of search terms, results were reviewed sequentially until all results on a page appeared irrelevant (saturation-based stopping rule).

Retrieved papers were screened in two stages. First, titles and abstracts were reviewed to determine whether the study measured survival or growth of *A. palmata* colonies or fragments with information on colony size. Second, papers passing the initial screen were read in full to assess whether they met criteria for demographic parameter estimation. Specifically, a study was included for survival if it reported the initial number and size of colonies or fragments and the number surviving over a defined interval amenable to annualization. A study was included for growth if it recorded initial and final sizes, or initial sizes and areal growth rates; studies reporting only linear extension of branches were excluded.

**Expanded search.** An expanded audit was conducted in March 2026 to verify completeness. This involved screening approximately 80 PDFs in the project literature library against inclusion criteria, querying a curated library of 137 papers via NotebookLM (Google), and searching PubMed and Semantic Scholar for additional candidate studies. Formal database searches identified ~2,518 records across PubMed (351), Web of Science (1,095 across 3 query sets), Elicit/Semantic Scholar (298), Google Scholar (~150), forward citation chaining from 12 included studies (381), and bioRxiv/EuropePMC preprints (23). The expanded PubMed search added 6 queries beyond the original Boolean strings, targeting genus-level terms, MeSH headings, abbreviated species names, ESA-listed species, and restoration-specific vocabulary. Web of Science searches included a broader *Acropora*+Caribbean query set and a threatened coral demography query. Scopus was not accessible and is documented as a limitation. After deduplication (~1,200--1,400 estimated unique records), 98 unique papers plus 3 data repositories (101 total) were assessed at full text. This expansion identified 31 new candidate papers from the initial audit phase, which were subjected to dual independent screening by two reviewers and independent audit of extracted data (see Section 2.2). An additional 13 papers were screened from the formal database and expanded searches (2026-03-29); all were excluded. The net result was the addition of three new studies (Rogers 1982; Rogers and Muller 2012; Ramos-Romero et al. 2025) and the removal of two previously included studies (Muller et al. 2008, excluded for imprecise survival data, absence of colony size information, and bleaching-confounded mortality; Sutherland et al. 2016, excluded because its contemporary survey component overlapped with NOAA monitoring and the EDR historical dataset tracked photostation quadrats rather than individually tagged colonies). One additional candidate (Ramos et al. 2024) was removed because the reported metric (recent mortality prevalence, i.e., the fraction of colonies showing any recent tissue loss) did not constitute whole-colony mortality.

**Inter-rater reliability.** To assess screening reliability, 31 candidate papers from the expanded search were independently screened by two reviewers. Raw agreement was 71% (Cohen's kappa = -0.148, reflecting the kappa paradox under highly skewed marginal distributions where both raters excluded most papers). Nine disagreements were adjudicated by an independent third review, resulting in the removal of two previously included studies and the addition of one new study. The final meta-analysis comprised k = 17 studies contributing 22 study-level effects (NOAA split into FL Keys, Curacao, and Navassa; Vardi 2011 split into Jamaica, Puerto Rico, and Virgin Gorda; Garrison & Ward 2008 split into control and relocated groups; Neely et al. 2022 added as a single FL Keys natural colony effect after direct data sharing), yielding a pooled annual survival of 78.0% (95% CI: 70.1-84.3%, I^2 = 97.2%). Roth et al. (2013) was excluded because it uses the same Haulover Bay colony data as Rogers & Muller (2012), as confirmed by the paper's explicit citation of and acknowledgments to the Rogers & Muller monitoring program.

**Inclusion criteria.** A study was included if it: (1) reported data on *A. palmata* specifically; (2) used a longitudinal design with individually tracked colonies or fragments; (3) reported survival proportion and sample size, or areal growth rates, over a defined time interval; and (4) did not overlap with data already in the compilation. Cross-sectional surveys of colony condition, partial mortality prevalence studies, laboratory micro-fragment experiments (<1 cm^2), and post-settlement recruit studies were excluded (see Supplementary Table S1 for the full exclusion list).

**NOAA data overlap.** The NOAA Acropora Demographic Monitoring Program (NCEI Accession 0142175) contributed the largest individual-level dataset (n = 4,048 observations across the Florida Keys, Curacao, and Navassa). Multiple published analyses draw on subsets of these same tagged colonies (e.g., Williams and Miller 2012; Bright 2013; Miller 2009). To prevent double-counting, NOAA program data were included once under a single study identifier, and all papers analyzing overlapping subsets were excluded as independent studies. The same logic was applied to USGS datasets: Chapron et al. (2023), which re-surveyed colonies from Kuffner et al. (2020), was excluded, and only the Kuffner et al. (2020) dataset was retained.

## 2.2 Data extraction and standardization

**Two data tiers.** Extracted data were organized into two tiers based on resolution. Tier 1 (individual-level) studies provided raw data files with individual colony identifiers, sizes at each census, and survival fates; these supported size-specific analyses via GAMs and GLMMs. Seven studies qualified as Tier 1, contributing ~7,800 individual survival records and ~6,300 growth records (Table 1). Tier 2 (summary-level) studies provided study-level survival proportions, sample sizes, and mean colony sizes extracted from published tables, figures, or text. Tier 2 study-level effects were compiled from summary data, of which 8 were hand-extracted by RD from the original publications and 3 were extracted from source PDFs during the expanded search (see below). Three studies were split into multiple effects: NOAA contributed 3 regional effects (FL Keys, Curacao, Navassa), Vardi (2011) contributed 3 regional effects (Jamaica, Puerto Rico, Virgin Gorda), and Garrison and Ward (2008) contributed 2 treatment-group effects (control colonies, relocated fragments).

**Extraction verification.** All 8 hand-extracted Tier 2 studies were verified against source PDFs. Four studies with tabulated data (Rosales et al. 2024, Forrester et al. 2013, Ortiz Prosper 2005, Maurer et al. 2022) were exact matches. Three studies with figure-estimated data (Garrison and Ward 2008, Williams and Miller 2010, Bruckner and Bruckner 2001) were internally consistent. Vardi 2011 (20 rows across 3 regions) was verified correct against dissertation Tables 4-1, 4-2, and 4-A3. Zero extraction errors were found.

**Size standardization.** All colony sizes were converted to live planar tissue area (cm^2), following the measurement protocol of Vardi et al. (2012): longest axis multiplied by longest perpendicular axis multiplied by the proportion of live tissue. For studies reporting only colony diameter (Mendoza-Quiroz et al. 2023), area was estimated assuming a circular footprint (pi * (d/2)^2). For studies reporting only fragment or colony length with no width data, empirical width-to-length ratios derived from the individual-level datasets were applied to estimate planar area. One study (Rogers and Muller 2012) reported size as volume (cm^3) with no conversion possible; size was set to missing but survival data were retained. Studies reporting 100% tissue cover or new settlers were assumed to have live area equal to skeletal area.

**Annualization.** Non-annual survival rates were annualized assuming a constant hazard rate: surv_annual = surv_raw^(1/t), where t is the observation interval in years. For Kaplan-Meier survival estimates (Ramos-Romero et al. 2025), t was set to the full monitoring duration rather than the time of the last mortality event, because KM survival is sustained through the censoring period.

**Disturbance handling.** All observation intervals were retained in the primary analysis regardless of disturbance exposure, because storms, disease, bleaching, cold events, and chronic stressors are part of the demographic regime facing *A. palmata* populations rather than exogenous noise. Disturbance context was then layered onto the data using a curated Caribbean event timeline that distinguishes acute events used for baseline-exclusion sensitivity analyses from chronic or indirect stressors retained as contextual exposure (Table S1). In the current synthesis, the timeline contains 18 curated events, of which 11 are classified as acute events excluded from a counterfactual baseline and 7 are retained as context-only chronic, biotic, or framework-mediated pressures. Sensitivity summaries compare all intervals with subsets excluding the Neely et al. (2022) 2014 disease intervals, excluding NOAA storm intervals, and excluding all baseline-exclusion events. Excluding baseline-exclusion events changed the pooled annual survival estimate from 75.9% to 75.6% (a shift of -0.4 percentage points), while excluding only the Neely 2014 disease intervals increased the pooled estimate to 78.6%. This tiered framework avoids the older asymmetry of removing only locally flagged catastrophes while leaving comparable unflagged regional disturbances in place, and it preserves the central interpretation that disturbance is a demographic regime to be analyzed, not simply filtered away.

**Disturbance audit and interaction analysis.** To make disturbance assignment reproducible, the prepared interval-level disturbance fields were rebuilt independently from the curated Caribbean timeline and collapsed to unique study-window records. The rebuilt overlap table matched the prepared data exactly and was summarized as a study-window disturbance audit in the Supplementary Materials (Table S2). We additionally fit size-by-disturbance interaction models to test whether the relationship between colony size and demographic performance differed across disturbance states, separating intervals with no curated disturbance, context-only chronic pressure, and acute baseline-exclusion events (Fig. S17). Primary interaction models were fit to natural colonies only to avoid confounding with restoration-fragment dynamics.

**Expanded search data extraction.** Three Tier 2 studies added during the expanded search (Rogers 1982; Rogers and Muller 2012; Ramos-Romero et al. 2025) were extracted from source PDFs. Quantitative values from text and tables were transcribed; values reported only in figures were visually estimated from rendered page images without digitization software, introducing reading error of approximately 1-5%. Every extracted value was independently verified against the source PDF. The verification identified errors in initially extracted studies: time intervals were corrected for Ramos-Romero et al. (2025), and Ramos et al. (2024) was removed entirely due to a conceptual misinterpretation of partial mortality prevalence as whole-colony survival. Expanded search rows are flagged in the data files and all verification results are documented in the Supplementary Materials.

**Size class definitions.** Colonies were assigned to five size classes based on live planar tissue area: SC1, 0-10 cm^2 (recruits); SC2, 10-100 cm^2 (small juveniles); SC3, 100-900 cm^2 (large juveniles); SC4, 900-4,000 cm^2 (subadults); and SC5, >4,000 cm^2 (reproductive adults). These breaks follow the structure of published *A. palmata* matrix models (Vardi 2011) with the original smallest class (0-100 cm^2) subdivided to separate recruits from small juveniles.

## 2.3 Meta-analysis

**Effect size.** Annual survival proportions were converted to proportional log-odds (PLO) effect sizes using the `escalc()` function in the metafor package (Viechtbauer 2010) in R. A Haldane correction of 0.5 was added to zero cells (studies with 0% or 100% survival) to avoid undefined log-odds. The PLO transformation stabilizes variances and allows back-transformation to the probability scale for interpretation.

**Random-effects model.** Pooled survival was estimated using a random-effects model fitted with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation and the Knapp-Hartung adjustment for confidence intervals and test statistics, which provides more appropriate coverage than the default Wald-type inference at small k (Knapp and Hartung 2003). Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using I^2 (percentage of total variability due to between-study variance), tau^2 (absolute between-study variance), and Cochran's Q test. Ninety-five percent prediction intervals were computed to characterize the range of survival expected in a new study setting.

**Moderator analyses.** Random-effects meta-regressions were fitted to test four candidate moderators of survival: population type (natural colony vs. restoration fragment; 10 natural effects, 12 restoration effects), log-transformed mean colony size (cm^2), study year, and Caribbean region. All moderator models used REML estimation with the Knapp-Hartung adjustment. These analyses were considered exploratory given the modest number of effects (k < 10 per moderator level for some comparisons); we report results but note that moderator analyses require approximately k >= 10 per level for defensible inference.

**Leave-one-out sensitivity.** To assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted by sequentially removing each study and re-fitting the random-effects model. Studies whose removal changed the pooled survival estimate by more than 2 percentage points were flagged as influential.

**Classification sensitivity.** Because certain studies could plausibly be classified as either natural or restoration populations (e.g., Garrison and Ward 2008 tracked both transplanted fragments and control colonies; Vardi 2011 monitored naturally occurring colonies at sites where restoration also occurred), we conducted a classification sensitivity analysis. Four scenarios were tested, varying the assignment of ambiguous studies between natural and restoration categories, and subgroup differences were re-estimated under each scenario. This analysis evaluated whether the natural-versus-restoration comparison was robust to classification uncertainty.

**Restoration subtype sensitivity.** Because the label "restoration fragment" pooled biologically distinct materials and intervention histories, we also decomposed restoration records into a small set of defensible subtypes using study metadata: nursery outplants, nursery outplants that were re-cemented after disturbance, outplanted colonies, and natural fragments. This subtype sensitivity analysis was treated as an interpretive refinement rather than a replacement for the main natural-versus-restoration comparison, and was used to distinguish generic restoration effects from subtype-specific demographic patterns (Fig. S19).

**Risk-of-bias sensitivity analysis.** To assess whether study quality influenced the pooled survival estimate, we conducted two analyses. First, we fitted a random-effects meta-regression with the adapted Newcastle-Ottawa Scale score (range 4--10; see Supplementary Materials) as a continuous moderator, using REML estimation and the Knapp-Hartung adjustment. Second, we re-estimated the pooled survival after excluding all studies scoring 5 or below on the 10-point scale (Moderate risk or higher). This threshold removed studies with the weakest designs while retaining sufficient effects for meta-analysis.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3+ using the metafor package (Viechtbauer 2010). Data and code are available at https://github.com/stier-lab/Detmer-2025-coral-parameters.

## 2.4 Certainty of evidence

Formal GRADE assessment was not applied because this is a vital rate compilation rather than a treatment-effect review. However, we narratively assess certainty across the five GRADE domains: (1) Risk of bias: mean score 6.9/10 across adapted Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (Low to Moderate); (2) Inconsistency: extreme (I^2 = 97.2%), largely attributable to variation in mortality definitions, geographic context, and colony size distributions; (3) Indirectness: low -- studies directly measured *A. palmata* survival in Caribbean reef environments; (4) Imprecision: the 95% prediction interval (39.5--95.1%) indicates that survival in a new study setting remains highly uncertain; (5) Publication bias: assessed via Egger's regression test for funnel plot asymmetry and Duval-Tweedie trim-and-fill. The standard random-effects model showed no significant asymmetry (z = 0.45, p = 0.655); however, the three-level model showed significant asymmetry (t = 3.47, p = 0.002), likely reflecting the nesting structure (regional sub-effects from NOAA and Vardi) rather than true publication bias. Trim-and-fill imputed 3 studies, shifting the pooled estimate from 79.4% to 77.0% (a shift of -2.4 percentage points). These results should be interpreted cautiously given k = 22 effects, and noting that vital rate compilations (where every tracked population contributes a survival estimate) differ from treatment-effect meta-analyses where non-significant results may go unpublished.
