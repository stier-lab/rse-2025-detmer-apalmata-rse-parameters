Dear Derek,

[PERSONALIZE — any shared connections, conferences, or prior interactions]

Congratulations on the Manzello et al. (2025) paper in *Science* — it's an extraordinary piece of work, both for the scale of the coordinated response and for the sobering clarity of the results. It's already reshaping how we think about *Acropora* population viability in the Caribbean.

I'm writing because my graduate student Raine Detmer and I are working on a project that we think complements your paper in a useful way, and I wanted to see if you might be able to help us connect with some of the monitoring programs that contributed data to your study.

**What we're building:** We've spent the past year compiling every published dataset on *A. palmata* survival and growth across the Caribbean — 17 studies, ~7,800 individual-level records, plus summary data from 10 additional studies. We're using these data to build a size-structured Lefkovitch population model that estimates how survival, growth, and fragmentation vary with colony size, and what that means for population viability and restoration target-setting. The manuscript is targeting *Coral Reefs* and follows PRISMA systematic review guidelines. All data and code are open on GitHub.

**Where your paper fits in:** Your dose-response data (ED50 = 7.8 DHW, ED95 = 17.6 DHW) let us project what happens to our population model under recurring catastrophic heatwaves at different return intervals. We've already built this analysis — and the results are striking. Even a 2023-scale event every 50 years drives quasi-extinction probability above 95%. But there's a critical assumption we can't test with the aggregate mortality data from your paper: **does the size-survival relationship collapse under extreme heat, or does it just shift downward?** Our 79 NOAA tagged colonies from 2023 suggest the size gradient persists but weakens (SC4 = 75% survival, SC5 = 61%), but that's too few colonies to be confident.

**What we're looking for:** Several of the programs that contributed to your paper — CRF, Mote, Mission: Iconic Reefs — track individual outplanted colonies with size measurements over time. If those programs have colony-level data with sizes (not just the alive/dead site-level counts from your paper), that would let us estimate size-dependent vital rates under both the chronic regime and the 2023 event. This would be a major advance — effectively bridging your event-scale analysis with process-based demographic modeling.

Would you be willing to introduce us to the relevant contacts at these programs? Or if you have a sense of which programs actually track individual colony sizes (vs. just site-level counts), that alone would help us target our outreach. We're offering co-authorship to any data contributors on the synthesis paper, plus site-specific population viability analyses that could be directly useful for restoration planning.

I'm happy to share our full dataset and analysis pipeline, or jump on a call if that would be easier than email. We genuinely believe this synthesis will be more useful with the restoration monitoring data included, and we want to make sure the contribution is recognized appropriately.

Thank you so much for considering this — and again, congratulations on an important paper.

All the best,
Adrian

Dear [Name],

I'm an ecologist at UC Santa Barbara, and I'm writing because my graduate student Raine Detmer and I are working on a project that I think could be genuinely useful for CRF's restoration planning — and where CRF's monitoring data would make the science much stronger.

**The project:** We're building the first comprehensive, size-structured population model for *Acropora palmata* using demographic data compiled from across the Caribbean. The goal is to answer practical questions that matter for restoration: How big do outplanted colonies need to grow before they have a good chance of long-term survival? How does the size-survival relationship change under different disturbance regimes? What's the minimum viable population size given realistic growth and mortality rates? We currently have ~7,800 individual-level survival records from 7 studies spanning Florida, the USVI, Mexico, the Dominican Republic, Jamaica, Puerto Rico, and the British Virgin Islands. The manuscript is targeting *Coral Reefs*.

**Why CRF's data matters:** Right now, our individual-level restoration data comes from just two published studies — Pausch et al. (2018), which used CRF fragments for a 2014-2016 experiment at two reef sites, and Kuffner et al. (2020), which tracked 52 USGS outplants in the Dry Tortugas. That's about 1,000 restoration records total. CRF's full monitoring database almost certainly dwarfs this — and it spans both the chronic disturbance regime and the 2023 heatwave, which is exactly the comparison our model needs.

**What we'd need:** Individual colony or fragment records with:
- A size measurement at each monitoring visit (total colony area, L×W, planar area — any measure of live tissue extent works; we handle standardization)
- Alive/dead status at each visit
- Site location and monitoring dates
- Colony origin information (genotype, outplant cohort, nursery source) if available

Any format you already maintain is fine — we do all the cleaning and standardization on our end and would share the standardized version back with you.

**What we offer in return:**
- **Co-authorship** on the synthesis paper for anyone at CRF who contributes substantially to the data or its interpretation. We're genuinely interested in collaboration, not just data extraction.
- **Site-specific population viability analyses** for CRF's restoration sites — our model can project population trajectories, estimate time-to-self-sustaining-population, and identify which vital rates matter most at each site.
- **Full access** to our compiled Caribbean-wide dataset and analysis pipeline (all open on GitHub).
- **The heatwave scenario analysis** — we've already parameterized a dose-response model from Manzello et al. (2025) that projects population dynamics under different heatwave return intervals. With CRF colony-level data, we could run this for specific sites and genotypes.

I realize this is a big ask, and I want to be transparent about how the data would be used. Every contributing dataset is cited and credited in the paper, with the data contributor listed as a co-author. We follow PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews, so the inclusion criteria, screening process, and data handling are all documented and reproducible. The raw data are stored securely and not redistributed without permission — only the standardized vital rate summaries are published.

I'd welcome the chance to talk more about this by phone or video if that would be helpful. I think there's real potential for this to be a productive collaboration.

Thank you for your time,
Adrian
